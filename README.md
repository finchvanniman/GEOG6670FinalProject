This project aimed to create an environmental risk score for Michigan counties based on hazardous waste treatment and storage locations, solid waste landfills, environmental contamination sites, and PFAS sites of interest. Then, the overall Social Vulnerability (SVI) Theme scores were added to the risk score as these can compound the effects felt in the area. Social vulnerability is are the demographic and socioeconomic variables that influce how an inividual or community responds to a disaster or other major event. Further, population averages were added to the risk score to include the number of people living in the area that the risk score impacts. The more people living in an area, the more of an imapct the risk score has on humans. # Data
The data for this project came from three different sources. Firstly, two Google Earth Engine layers were utilized. These were for population and for county boundaries. Both of these layers are raster layers and provided the spatial mapping basis for this work. They intially covered the entire U.S., but were filtered to only include Michigan. The mean of the population was used for analysis. Then, the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) data came from the Center for Disease Control and Prevention. This was available for the year 2022 at Michigan county levels as a .csv file which was then joined to the counties. The SPL_THEMES were used in this analysis as they sum the scores from all four themes within the index. This provides a greater overview of the vulnerability. Lastly, the data layers for the hazardous waste, solid waste, PFAS, and environmental contamination site counts were found on the Michigan Open GIS Portal. Geojsons were donwloaded and utilized.
# Discussion of Results 
Through the duckdb analysis, the descriptive statistics were calculated. This shows that minimum and maximum values for each of the three different evnironmental risk scores and these are noted below in the discussion of each map. It also provides the average score across all of the counties. For the Environmental Risk, the average score is 258.17 which is on the lower end. For the Environmental Risk with SVI, the average score is 336.56 which is also on the lower end of the scale. Finally, for Environmental Risk with SVI and Population, the average score is 662.56 and again falls onto the lower portion of the scale. These averages show that there are a few outlier counties that are much higher on the scale than most of the counties. Looking at the quartile divisions, this is supported. For Envioronmental Risk, 75% of the data falls below the value of 215.25. For Environmental Risk with SVI, 75% of the data falls below the value of 282.90. For Environmental Risk with SVI and Population, 75% of the data falls below the value of 560.12. Areas of outliers can be seen through the maps created and the histograms following the maps. The areas of outliers and clusters of lower scores are very noticable within the histogram. Detroit is one area to note. 
The first map created titled 'Environmental Risk of Michigan Counties' shows the calculated environmental risk score of only the hazardous waste, solid waste, PFAS, and environmental contamination site counts. This score is from 12 to 3830. Highest areas are on the east side of the state near the highly populated areas of Detroit. There are also some areas with higher scores surrounding the area, as well as directly across on the west side of the state near Kalamazoo and Grand Rapids. The entire upper penninsula is in the lowest classification group of the scores. 
The second map created titled 'Environmental Risk with SVI of Michigan Counties' shows the calculated environmental risk score of the first map with the addition of SVI data as a factor in the score. When added, it was multiplied by 10 to show the importance of it. This score is from 45.59 to 3948.53. This map appears the exact same as the first map. The values within the classification has changed, however, each county falls within the same classification as it did in the first map. This is an interesting and unexpected result. This shows that the SVI scores of each county aligns with the environmental score calculation and therefore relates to the amount of risk carried in each county. So, it can be assumed that areas with higher SVI scores have higher environmental risk (and vice versa) based on these maps and the lack of change that results. 
The final map created titled 'Environmental Risk with SVI and Population Density of Michigan Counties' shows the calculated environmental risk score of the first map with the addition of both the SVI data and population density as a factor. The scores here vary from 46.51 to 8638.64. This map does provide slightly different results from the previous two. It further reflects the number of individuals who are impacted by the environmental risk score calculated. Several counties on the east side of the state near Detroit changed into a different category. Most moved into a lower score classification. On the west side of the state, counties also moved into a lower classification. The movement into lower classification shows that there are less individuals in the areas which are being impacted by the high environmental risk score. 
Throughout all three map results, there are no changes in the Upper Penninsula. Even without the addition of population, the areas of the state which have higher levels of population are highlighted as having higher environmental risk score. This displays a relationship between population and higher environmental issues and concerns.
